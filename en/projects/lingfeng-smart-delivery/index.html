Project Overview

Lingfeng Smart Delivery was an air-ground collaborative delivery concept for semi-enclosed environments such as campuses and residential communities. The proposed service combined an autonomous ground vehicle, a drone, and a WeChat Mini Program: the ground vehicle handled longer routes on flat roads, the drone extended delivery to upper floors, windows, or corridors, and the Mini Program connected ordering, route visibility, and delivery feedback. I served as CAO in a 15-person interdisciplinary team, focusing on market research, competitive analysis, market strategy, and risk assessment while helping translate the technical concept into target-market, positioning, and commercialization choices.

Pain Points & Opportunities

The problem was broader than delivery speed. First, peak demand and access restrictions could overwhelm human delivery and make arrival times unreliable. Second, ground-only autonomous vehicles depended on accessible roads and could not reach elevated or precise last-mile destinations. Third, delivery inside a campus or residential compound was shaped by site access, safety, and institutional approval: the product had to work for students and residents while also earning permission from a school, property manager, or logistics partner. The opportunity was therefore to connect complementary transport modes to a workable service system rather than simply add another device.

Goals & Metrics

User Research

I separated the market into two connected groups: schools, property managers, and logistics companies that controlled access and purchasing decisions, and students and residents whose behavior determined whether the service would be used. The competitive review covered delivery drones, autonomous delivery vehicles, delivery robots, and human delivery as an alternative, comparing reach, terrain constraints, precision at the destination, safety, customization, price sensitivity, and barriers to entry. The source material also applied Five Forces to suppliers, buyers, entrants, substitutes, and industry rivals. This analysis led to an opportunity for ground vehicles to handle longer internal routes, drones to cover vertical and precise delivery, and each deployment to be adapted to the site. Solution Design

The findings were translated into three layers. The positioning emphasized air-ground collaboration, site customization, safety, efficiency, and user-centered service: the Mini Program accepted orders and displayed delivery information, the ground vehicle covered flat-road transport, and the drone handled the final precise leg. The proposed B2B+B2C model included device sales or leasing, platform fees, order commissions, and route-design fees for institutions, plus order payments, memberships, and incentives for individuals. The go-to-market proposal combined introductory penetration pricing and direct institutional outreach with online communication and offline demonstrations. These were project-stage proposals, not evidence of realized revenue.

Execution Plan

I turned fragmented industry information, user scenarios, and competing approaches into product decisions. I first separated site partners from end users, then used competitor categories and Five Forces to identify differentiation, and finally mapped those conclusions to positioning, business model, pricing, channels, promotion, and risk responses. The technical concept was developed jointly by team members from computer science, artificial intelligence, software, mechanical engineering, and business disciplines. My focus was aligning air-ground collaboration, computer vision, FPGA, and Mini Program capabilities with real usage contexts, customer decisions, and a plausible commercial path. Iteration Reflection

The project taught me that a complete business plan is not the same as a validated product. The proposal covered market, competition, positioning, marketing, and financial projections, but a stronger next phase would test four questions first: whether institutional decision makers would open their sites and accept the partnership cost, whether end users would pay for delivery, whether air-ground collaboration materially outperformed simpler ground-only or human services, and whether regulation and unit economics supported continued operation. A focused campus MVP, decision-maker interviews, end-user tests, a service blueprint, and a real cost model would provide stronger evidence than broad market assumptions and long-range projections.
